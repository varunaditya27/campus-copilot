# Research, Projects, and Innovation

> **Demo corpus:** The programs and processes below are fictionalized workshop material.

## Why Projects Matter

Engineering projects connect classroom concepts with open-ended problems. A strong project usually has a clear problem statement, a reason the problem matters, a measurable objective, a technically defensible approach, and evidence that the system works.

## Project Lifecycle

A useful generic lifecycle is:

```text
Problem discovery
      ↓
Literature / background study
      ↓
Requirements
      ↓
Architecture
      ↓
Prototype
      ↓
Evaluation
      ↓
Iteration
      ↓
Demonstration
      ↓
Documentation
```

Students should avoid starting with a technology and searching for a problem afterward. "We used an LLM" is not a problem statement. A better project explains what limitation exists, why existing approaches are insufficient, and what the proposed system contributes.

## Research-Oriented Projects

A research project should make a question testable. Examples include:

- comparing retrieval strategies for RAG;
- evaluating hallucination reduction techniques;
- measuring the effect of chunking strategies on retrieval quality;
- comparing lightweight models for campus-assistant workloads;
- studying human approval patterns in agentic workflows.

The assistant can help students formulate hypotheses and experiment plans, but claims should be supported by actual measurements.

## Building a Good Technical Project

A strong student project should document:

### Problem

What problem is being solved?

### Users

Who experiences the problem?

### Constraints

What limits the solution? Examples include latency, cost, privacy, hardware, or data availability.

### Architecture

What components are required, and why?

### Evaluation

How will success be measured?

### Limitations

What does the system not solve?

## AI Project Evaluation

For an AI assistant, useful evaluation dimensions include:

- retrieval relevance;
- answer correctness;
- citation accuracy;
- latency;
- tool-call success rate;
- failure handling;
- human approval behavior;
- cost per interaction.

A polished interface is useful, but it should not substitute for evaluation.
