# Research, Projects, and Innovation

## Why Projects Matter

Engineering coursework builds foundations; independent and team projects are where students learn to work on open-ended problems that do not come with a known answer at the back of the textbook. A strong project has a clearly stated problem, a reason the problem matters, a measurable objective, a technically defensible approach, and evidence that the resulting system actually works. Across all five departments, project work is woven into the curriculum through course mini-projects, a mandatory capstone project in the final year, and voluntary research assistantships with faculty.

## Project Lifecycle

Faculty advisors generally expect student projects to move through a recognizable set of stages: problem discovery, a literature or background study to understand what already exists, a requirements phase, an architecture or design phase, a working prototype, an evaluation against the original requirements, one or more rounds of iteration based on that evaluation, a demonstration, and finally documentation. Projects that skip the background study or the evaluation stage are the most common source of weak capstone defenses, according to feedback compiled by department project coordinators each year.

Students are advised against starting with a particular technology and searching afterward for a problem it can solve. A project proposal that only says "we built a system using a popular new technique" is not a problem statement. A stronger proposal explains what limitation exists in current approaches, why those approaches fall short, and what the proposed project specifically contributes that is new or better.

## Research Centres

Students looking to get involved in ongoing faculty research, beyond their own course or capstone projects, can approach one of the university's six research centres directly: the Centre for AI and Data Systems, the Embedded Systems and IoT Lab, the Cybersecurity Research Lab, the Robotics and Automation Lab, the Power and Renewable Energy Lab, and the Materials and Manufacturing Lab. Each centre is headed by a faculty coordinator who reviews applications from undergraduate students each semester for research-assistantship positions, typically requiring a short statement of interest and, for later-semester students, a transcript. Positions are unpaid but carry credit toward the university's undergraduate research recognition scheme, and students who complete at least two semesters of research assistantship work are eligible to be listed as contributing authors on any resulting publication, subject to the supervising faculty member's judgment of their contribution.

## Building a Good Technical Project

Faculty reviewers and capstone evaluation panels consistently look for a project report to address the following, regardless of department or subject area:

### Problem

What specific problem is the project solving, and why does it matter?

### Users

Who experiences this problem in practice, and how was that understood — through interviews, existing literature, or direct observation?

### Constraints

What limits the space of viable solutions? Common constraints cited in student projects include cost, available hardware, processing latency, data availability, and safety or regulatory requirements.

### Architecture

What are the major components of the system, and why was this particular structure chosen over alternatives?

### Evaluation

How is success measured? A project report should state what was measured, how it was measured, and what the results actually showed — including where the system fell short.

### Limitations

What does the system not solve, and what would extending it require?

## The Undergraduate Capstone Project

Every student completes a two-semester capstone project in their final year, undertaken individually or in teams of up to four, under the supervision of a faculty guide from their home department. Capstone teams are expected to register their project title and guide with their department's project coordinator by the second week of the seventh semester, present a mid-term progress review before an internal faculty panel partway through the eighth semester, and defend the completed project before a panel that includes at least one external examiner from industry or another institution. Capstone projects judged to be of exceptional quality by the department panel are nominated for the university's annual Best Capstone Project award, presented each year at the Annual Technical Fest's project exhibition.

## Presenting and Publishing Work

Students whose project work produces a genuine technical contribution are encouraged, with their faculty guide's support, to submit it to an appropriate student or academic conference, or to present it at the university's own project exhibitions held during the Annual Technical Fest and at department day celebrations. The relevant research centre or department office can advise on suitable venues and, where available, provide limited travel support for students presenting accepted work externally.
