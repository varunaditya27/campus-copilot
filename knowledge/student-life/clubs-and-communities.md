# Clubs, Communities, and Student Activities

## Why Student Communities Matter

Formal coursework builds foundations; student clubs are where most students turn those foundations into practice. A good technical community gives its members a reason to build things outside of graded assignments, a peer group that asks harder questions than a syllabus does, and a track record of workshops, competitions, and projects to point to when applying for internships or further study. The Dean of Student Affairs' office recognizes and funds student communities through the Student Activity Centre, and most clubs report a steady rise in membership between the second and sixth semesters as students look for ways to differentiate their resumes.

Across the four recognized technical communities, total active membership runs to roughly 950 students in a typical year — a little under a third of the undergraduate body — with the Coding Club alone accounting for close to 400 of that figure.

## Technical Communities

### Coding Club

The Coding Club is the largest and oldest of the four communities, founded in the university's third year of operation and now numbering close to 400 active members drawn from all five departments, though Computer Science and Engineering and Information Science and Engineering students make up the majority. The club runs weekly problem-solving sessions every Wednesday evening in Computing Lab 1, rotating through topics such as data structures, dynamic programming, and interview-style problem sets. It also fields teams for external competitive-programming contests and runs an internal rating ladder that ranks members by contest performance across the semester.

Notable past activities include a 30-hour internal hackathon held each January in the Innovation and Computing Centre, an open-source contribution drive each October timed to coincide with global open-source events, and a beginner-friendly "Code in 30 Days" bootcamp run at the start of every odd semester that has onboarded over 200 first-year students since it began. The club is run by a student core team of twelve — a president, a vice-president, and ten leads across sub-verticals including competitive programming, web development, and open-source — elected each April for a one-year term.

### AI and Machine Learning Community

The AI and Machine Learning Community, with roughly 220 active members, focuses on machine learning fundamentals, applied projects, and emerging developments in the field. It runs a structured reading group every alternate Friday that works through a foundational paper or technique, model-building workshops held in the Innovation Lab roughly once a month, and an annual dataset challenge each March where teams of three to four compete to build the best-performing model on a released dataset, judged by a panel of two faculty advisors from the Centre for AI and Data Systems. Past challenges have covered image classification, sentiment analysis, and time-series forecasting problems.

The community also maintains a mentorship track pairing third- and fourth-year members with first- and second-year students who are new to machine learning, with pairings reviewed and refreshed each semester.

### Cybersecurity Community

The Cybersecurity Community has around 180 active members and focuses on secure software development, network fundamentals, ethical security research, and security awareness on campus. It runs monthly capture-the-flag practice sessions in the Cybersecurity Lab, working through categories such as web exploitation, cryptography, and reverse engineering, and fields a team for external inter-collegiate CTF competitions each year. The community also runs an annual campus-wide security awareness week each November, in coordination with the university's IT Services department, covering topics such as phishing recognition and password hygiene through posters, short talks, and a simulated phishing exercise open to volunteering students and staff.

Membership requires completing a short foundational track — three introductory sessions covering ethics, legal boundaries, and basic tooling — before a student can participate in CTF practice, a requirement the community's faculty advisor put in place after an early incident involving unauthorized scanning of campus network infrastructure.

### Robotics and Embedded Systems Community

The Robotics and Embedded Systems Community has close to 150 active members and works on microcontrollers, sensors, robotics platforms, embedded programming, computer vision, and hardware-software integration. It operates out of the Embedded Systems and IoT Lab and runs a build cycle each semester culminating in a demonstration event, where teams present functioning hardware projects to faculty judges and fellow members. Past projects have included a line-following robot competition series, a semester-long drone-building project culminating in a supervised outdoor flight demonstration, and a home-automation project that was later adopted as a teaching example in a second-year Electronics and Communication Engineering laboratory course.

Because lab access involves shared equipment and component budgets, the community caps active project-team membership at 60 students per semester, admitted through a short interest form and a brief conversation with a student lead; students not selected for a project team in a given semester are welcome to attend general meetings and join the following cycle.

## Joining a Club

New students are introduced to all four communities during Orientation Week, when each club runs an introductory stall and information session in the Student Activity Centre. Outside of orientation, a student interested in joining a club should:

1. attend one of the club's regular weekly or biweekly sessions, which are open to visitors regardless of membership status;
2. speak with a student lead or core-team member about current projects and expectations;
3. fill out the club's membership form, available at the Student Activity Centre help desk or from any core-team member;
4. participate in an initial activity, workshop, or small project to get oriented;
5. for clubs with capped or competitive tracks (such as Cybersecurity's foundational track or Robotics' project teams), complete any additional onboarding the club requires.

There is no membership fee for any of the four technical communities; each club receives an annual budget from the Student Activity Centre based on membership size and the previous year's activity report.

## Organizing Club Events

Clubs proposing a workshop, competition, or larger event follow a standard process coordinated through the Student Activity Centre: an event proposal naming the organizing club, expected attendance, and venue requirements is submitted at least two weeks in advance, venue availability is confirmed with the relevant facility office (the Sports Office for outdoor space, the Central Library for the Seminar Hall, and so on), and the event is then opened for student registration and promoted through campus noticeboards, department mailing lists, and the Student Activity Centre calendar. Clubs organizing an event that draws external participants from other colleges must additionally route the proposal through the Dean of Student Affairs' office for approval, typically a further week in advance.

## What Students Gain

Beyond the specific technical skills each community teaches, active club participation is consistently cited by the Placement Cell as a differentiator in student profiles, particularly for roles that value initiative and teamwork. Regular activity in a club can help a student build technical depth in a focus area, practice public speaking and event coordination, learn to plan and budget a project or event from scratch, build a portfolio of tangible work to discuss in interviews, and form a peer network that often persists well beyond graduation.
