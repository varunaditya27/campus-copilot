# Student Wellbeing and Support

## A Healthy Campus Experience

University life brings together academic workload, project deadlines, lab commitments, extracurricular activities, and personal responsibilities, often all at once. The university encourages students to build sustainable routines rather than treat exhaustion as a mark of how hard they're working — sleep, regular meals, and time away from screens are not optional extras around a degree; they're part of what makes a demanding semester survivable.

Class representatives, hostel wardens, and department faculty advisors are all trained to notice when a student seems to be struggling and to point them toward the right support, so students don't need to navigate every difficulty entirely on their own.

## When Academic Work Feels Overwhelming

Students who feel like they're falling behind are encouraged to name the specific problem rather than let it stay a vague sense of being overwhelmed. Common underlying causes include:

- difficulty understanding a particular concept or course;
- a workload that has become genuinely excessive for the semester;
- poor time management rather than an unreasonable course load;
- a pattern of missed deadlines that has started to snowball;
- unclear expectations about what a course or assignment actually requires;
- difficulty balancing coursework against extracurricular or hostel commitments.

The first stop for an academic difficulty is usually the course instructor or the department's academic advisor, both of whom hold weekly office hours listed on the department noticeboard. The Central Library's peer-tutoring desk and department-run doubt-clearing sessions, typically held in the week before internal assessments, are also available for students who want structured help outside class.

## Building a Realistic Schedule

Students who feel behind often benefit from working through a simple planning cycle rather than trying to fix everything at once:

1. List everything currently on your plate — coursework, projects, club commitments, and personal obligations.
2. Estimate roughly how much time each item actually needs, not how much time you wish it needed.
3. Identify which deadlines are truly fixed and which have some flexibility.
4. Prioritize the highest-impact work first, rather than the easiest or most recent item.
5. Block out focused work periods on a calendar, including buffer time for things that run long.
6. Review progress every few days and adjust the plan rather than abandoning it the first time it slips.

A schedule that leaves no room for sleep, meals, commuting, or the inevitable unexpected task is not a realistic plan and tends to collapse within a week. The Dean of Student Affairs office runs a short time-management workshop each semester, usually in the third week, open to any student who wants a structured introduction to this kind of planning.

## Peer Support and Community

Friends, classmates, hostel mates, mentors, and student clubs are often the first and most natural source of support during a difficult stretch of the semester. Departmental student associations and hostel communities are encouraged to look out for members who seem to be withdrawing or struggling, and to gently point them toward a hostel warden, faculty advisor, or the Dean of Student Affairs office when a problem seems bigger than peer support alone can handle.

## When to Seek Additional Help

Some situations call for more than a study plan or a conversation with a friend — a health concern, a family emergency, a safety issue, or a period of prolonged stress or low mood that isn't improving on its own. In these situations, students are strongly encouraged to reach out promptly rather than wait for things to resolve by themselves.

The university's Student Counselling Centre, located on the first floor of the Student Activity Centre, offers confidential one-on-one sessions with qualified counsellors on a walk-in basis during weekday mornings, or by appointment booked through the Student Services Desk. Sessions are free of charge for enrolled students and are kept strictly confidential; nothing discussed in a counselling session is shared with faculty, family, or the Registrar's Office without the student's consent, except where the counsellor judges there to be an immediate safety risk.

Hostel residents can also approach their hostel warden at any hour for an urgent personal or safety concern, and the university's 24-hour campus security desk, reachable from any campus landline by dialing 100, can reach emergency medical or security assistance at any time of day or night. For a medical emergency, the campus health centre near the Sports Complex is staffed around the clock during term time.

Students supporting a friend through a difficult time are encouraged to listen without judgment, avoid trying to solve a problem that's beyond their expertise, and help connect their friend to the Student Counselling Centre, a hostel warden, or the Dean of Student Affairs office rather than carrying the situation alone.

## Privacy and Personal Information

Students should be cautious about sharing highly sensitive personal information — medical records, financial documents, passwords, or similar material — through informal or unsecured channels, including casual messages to classmates or unofficial group chats. Official university offices such as the Financial Aid Office and the Student Counselling Centre have secure processes for handling sensitive documentation, and students with questions about how their information is handled are welcome to ask the relevant office directly.
